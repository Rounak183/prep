## 2. Spain-remote is now a worse idea than it looked — and your €150k instinct there was right

Two things I had wrong.

**You'd need a different permit.** Your current authorisation is tied to your employer, and on the new Reglamento (RD 1155/2024, in force May 2025) an initial permit is limited to one occupation and one autonomous community for the first year — *unless* you were sponsored as a highly-qualified professional, in which case that limitation doesn't apply. **Check your resolution letter for which sub-type you hold; it changes how free you already are.** At renewal it becomes a 4-year, work-anywhere-in-Spain, any-employer authorisation.

Working remotely for a *foreign* employer isn't permitted on that permit at all. The right route is the **teletrabajo internacional** authorisation (Startups Law, art. 74 bis): applied for from inside Spain via the UGE, **3 years**, resolved in **20 days with positive silence**, income bar around €34k. Migration-wise that's clean and easy.

**But the tax hit is the real cost, and it's why your €150k instinct for Spain-remote was sound.** The famous "Beckham Law" doesn't apply to you — Gipuzkoa runs its own foral system under the Concierto Económico. What you're almost certainly on is **Norma Foral 3/2014 art. 56 bis**: a **30% exemption on gross employment income for eleven tax periods**. At your salary that's worth roughly **€9k–€10k a year**, and it runs far longer than Beckham's six years. It's also *better* than Beckham would be at this income, because the flat 24% allows no deductions.

The catch: art. 56 bis requires the employer to be **resident in Spain or a Spanish permanent establishment**. The state regime was amended in 2023 to cover remote work for foreign employers; **the Gipuzkoan text was not.** So a remote role for a foreign company, while you stay in San Sebastián, is the one move that plausibly destroys the exemption — and there's no state Beckham to fall back on.

Which is exactly why Spain-remote needs a big number: **your break-even there is ~€98k gross**, and the EU-remote market mostly can't clear it. Grafana Spain tops out at €142k at the 75th percentile; Celonis Madrid is €56k–€78k; Elastic Spain €74k–€98k; Hugging Face and ElevenLabs both run geo-tiered local benchmarking despite being remote-first. The cleanest evidence: Elastic pays €74k–€98k in Spain and €160k–€166k in Amsterdam for the same work.

**Also worth knowing: moving to Madrid or Barcelona would put you on state IRPF and you could not claim Beckham** (it requires five years' prior non-residence in Spain, which you'd fail). So a Madrid role is ordinary state tax — worse than your current position unless the gross is much higher.

**Net effect: I'm dropping the entire "no visa needed, Spain-remote" tier from wave one.** It was the centrepiece of yesterday's plan and it was wrong — built on the assumption you had independent work rights and would keep your tax position. Grafana, Celonis, Elastic and Datadog Madrid come off the priority list.

**Two things worth doing this week, before any application:** confirm which permit sub-type you hold, and confirm with a Gipuzkoan asesor whether you currently hold the art. 56 bis exemption and from which tax year. The second is worth ~€10k/year and shapes every offer comparison you'll make.

## 4. Your two-week freshness rule: right instinct, needs one carve-out

I pulled real posting dates from the ATS APIs. Two findings.

**First, the mechanism.** Greenhouse's `updated_at` — the field most job aggregators show — is worthless. Monzo's Senior Platform Engineer shows `updated_at` of July 2026 and was **first published 20 March 2025**. Grafana bulk-rewrote 43 reqs on 18 August, making months-old postings look new. The real field is **`first_published`, available on the per-job endpoint**: `boards-api.greenhouse.io/v1/boards/{token}/jobs/{id}`. Lever gives `createdAt`, Ashby gives `publishedAt`. That's how to enforce your rule mechanically.

**Second, and this is the problem.** Applying the rule literally deletes most of your target list. Boards with **zero** engineering roles first published in the last 14 days: Monzo, DRW, Tower Research, Jump Trading, Hudson River Trading, XTX Markets, Flow Traders, xAI, Palantir, ElevenLabs, LiveKit, LangChain, MongoDB.

And some ages are extreme: **Palantir's Apollo Platform req was created in August 2022.** ElevenLabs' full-stack roles are 550–760 days old. Perplexity's backend roles are 400–540 days old. Figma's AI Product role — the one I flagged as a great level match — was first published **June 2025**.

So the rule needs splitting:

**Apply it strictly to companies with discrete headcount.** A 200-day-old req at Figma or Synthesia usually means the role is filled, frozen, or the board is stale. Skipping those is correct and saves real time.

**Ignore it entirely at continuous-hiring firms.** Jane Street's posting literally says "We're always hiring Software Engineers for all of our offices." Citadel, HRT, G-Research, Optiver and Palantir run perpetual requisitions — the posting date carries no information about whether they'd hire you next month. Monzo's own ad says "there's no closing date." A two-week filter would remove Jane Street, Citadel, HRT and Palantir from your search, which is the opposite of what you want given your brand criteria.

**Practical rule:** freshness filter on product companies, evergreen assumption at quant firms and frontier labs, and re-check `first_published` before writing any cover note.

## 5. On reaching out to sales and marketing people

Your reasoning is right: engineers at these companies get hammered on LinkedIn and sales people don't. Reply rates will be better. But I think you're optimising the wrong variable, and it's worth being precise about why.

**What a referral is actually worth depends on what goes in the recommendation box.** The ~28% referral conversion belongs to referrals where someone describes your work. A sales rep who has never seen your code writes "seemed impressive on a call" — and recruiters discount that to roughly the value of a cold application. Some ATSs now route unsubstantiated referrals identically. So the higher reply rate buys you a weaker artifact.

**But that doesn't mean don't contact them. It means ask for the right thing.** A salesperson or marketer can't endorse your engineering. They can do three things an engineer often won't bother with:

1. **Name the recruiter or hiring manager for that req.** This is the highest-value thing anyone can give you, and it costs them thirty seconds.
2. **Forward you internally** — "this person reached out, seems worth a look" to the TA team. That's not a referral, it's a warm intro, and it lands better than a cold application.
3. **Tell you whether the req is real** — whether headcount is approved, whether they're actually interviewing, what the band is. Given what the freshness data just showed about four-year-old postings, that intel is worth a lot.

So: **ask them for a name or a forward, not a referral.** "Who's recruiting for req X on the platform team?" converts far better from a salesperson than "will you refer me", because it's answerable and costs them nothing.

**The group you're both under-weighting: internal recruiters.** They are paid to fill these reqs, they're spammed by agencies rather than candidates, and they can move you straight to a screen with no intermediary. For elite quant especially the recruiting teams are small and genuinely responsive. A well-targeted note to the TA person who owns the req beats three referrals from strangers.

**Two things that change the calculus for your specific target list:**

- **At quant firms, referrals barely matter.** Jane Street runs 4–5 rounds of probability, mental math, market-making and estimation. The published process makes no mention of referrals — the filter is the test. Your 700+ LeetCode is the actual asset. Every hour spent networking at Jane Street is an hour not spent on prep, and prep is what decides it.
- **At frontier labs, a public artifact beats a referral.** Anthropic's careers page says roughly half their technical staff had no prior ML experience and explicitly invites blog posts and open-source contributions. Nothing about referrals. The Template Agents writeup is worth more to them than any introduction — and write it yourself, they ask candidates not to use AI in applications.

**And the thing no referral fixes:** every Anthropic London req is Staff or Staff+, DeepMind's agentic-tooling role asks 8+ years. A referral gets your CV read. It does not close a levelling gap. Where you're two levels light, the answer is a stronger artifact and a better interview, not a warmer introduction.

### Where I'd actually put the outreach hours

| Effort | Where | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Prep** | Jane Street, Citadel, HRT, Optiver, XTX, G-Research | The screen decides. Networking is near-worthless here. |
| **Public artifact** | Anthropic, OpenAI, DeepMind, xAI | They read the work. Write the Template Agents design note. |

| **Sales/marketing contacts** | Big tech and scale-ups | For names, forwards and req intel — not for referrals. |
| **Engineer referrals** | Google, Meta, Amazon, Microsoft, Stripe | The only place a formal referral programme materially helps, and where levelling is the real fight anyway. |

Keep the alumni angle, but stop treating it as an IIIT-Delhi channel specifically. The useful filter is *anyone with a reason to reply* — shared school, shared employer, shared conference, someone whose talk you actually watched. That's a much larger pool than one institution, and it's the property that makes the message get read.
